I recently finished BlueDot's [Technical AI Safety](https://bluedot.org/courses/technical-ai-safety) course.
The reading list is long. Read one unit at a time, it is easy to lose the shape of the whole.

So I did what one does with a pile of components. I laid all 99 readings out along the system they belong to: the lifecycle of a model, from the data it learns from to the point where it is served and retrained.
What came out of it is fairly simple.

> The readings crowd the middle of the lifecycle, where a model is shaped and examined, and thin out at both ends. Most of the open problems, wherever they sit, are problems of observation.

To be clear, that last claim (and the title) is my rough estimate. It rests on the readings of one course: BlueDot's Technical AI Safety course, which I recently completed and which is very professionally run. A different reading list might well draw a different map.

I spent over 50 hours reading the resources, and kept reading notes as I went. The numbers in brackets point to the references at the end. Each course reading keeps its number from those notes, from 1 to 99, so \[[4](#r4)\] always means the same METR post. Anything from outside the course is numbered from 100 onwards.

Fifty-one of the readings are mandatory. Eighty-one are about the technical work, and the remaining 18 are about careers and research directions.
The list is very recent. Sixty readings are from 2025 or 2026, and only five predate 2022.
It also leans on the labs. A quarter of it was written by a frontier lab, including the longest documents on the list, the safety frameworks and system cards \[[44](#r44), [45](#r45), [46](#r46), [50](#r50), [51](#r51), [52](#r52), [53](#r53), [54](#r54)\].

The last panel is the one this post is about. Evaluation alone holds 33 readings.
Pretraining holds none.

Figure 2 plots the 81 technical readings on those two axes. Each dot is a reading, numbered as in the references, and coloured by where it sits on the lifecycle. The scores are a first pass, so the exact positions should be taken loosely.

#### Ninety-nine readings on one loop

Let us draw the lifecycle of a model as a loop, similar to Chip Huyen's picture of a machine learning system \[[100](#r100)\]. Data flows forward to deployment, and monitoring feeds new data back in.
Her later book adds the stages that foundation models bring with them, such as post-training, prompting, retrieval and agents \[[101](#r101)\]. I filled in the training stages from the [Llama 3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21783) and [Tülu 3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15124) reports \[[102](#r102), [103](#r103)\]. That gives eight phases and 37 stages.

I then tagged every reading with the one stage it is mainly about. The tags are rough. A reading on evaluation often says something about deployment too.
But the overall picture does not change much. Interpretability alone gets 15 readings \[[56](#r56), [57](#r57), [58](#r58), [59](#r59), [60](#r60), [61](#r61), [62](#r62), [63](#r63), [64](#r64), [65](#r65), [69](#r69), [70](#r70), [71](#r71), [72](#r72), [73](#r73)\].
Thirteen of the 37 stages get no reading of their own. Six are not touched by any reading at all: data provenance, mid-training, downstream fine-tuning, inference optimisation, user feedback and model updates.

The middle is well read. On post-training, the course covers the limits of RLHF \[[17](#r17)\], Constitutional AI \[[19](#r19)\], and two routes to scalable oversight, debate \[[27](#r27), [28](#r28)\] and weak-to-strong generalisation \[[30](#r30)\]. The failure these guard against is already here, with recent models patching tests and graders while saying they knew it was unintended \[[4](#r4)\]. Evaluation gets the most pages, from alignment faking \[[36](#r36)\] and blackmail in a simulated office \[[38](#r38)\] to a re-run of the shutdown experiments that found clearer instructions removed most of the resistance \[[34](#r34)\]. Interpretability has its findings \[[71](#r71), [72](#r72), [69](#r69)\] and its critics \[[59](#r59), [62](#r62), [63](#r63)\] side by side.

The two ends are thin. At the front, there are three readings on filtering training data \[[11](#r11), [12](#r12), [13](#r13)\] and one on poisoning it, where about 250 documents planted a backdoor at every model size tested \[[14](#r14)\]. Nothing covers where the data comes from \[[104](#r104)\], and pretraining has no reading of its own. At the back, the course covers guardrails \[[55](#r55), [77](#r77)\] and AI control \[[74](#r74), [75](#r75)\], but says little about the rest of serving. A little fine-tuning can strip safety training \[[106](#r106), [107](#r107)\], retrieved documents can carry instructions an agent will follow \[[108](#r108)\], and a model can turn harmful once quantised \[[109](#r109)\]. The loop also closes through users. GPT-4o became sycophantic after an update leaned on thumbs-up feedback \[[110](#r110)\], and GPT-4 drifted on one simple task from 84% to 51% accuracy between two releases \[[111](#r111)\].
Huyen spends two whole chapters on what happens after deployment \[[100](#r100)\].
The course has no reading on it.

**Framing and governance**

<span id="op1"></span>**OP1. Threshold calls under uncertain evals.** Every frontier framework commits to act when a capability threshold is crossed, but none says much about how confident the measurement must be \[[44](#r44), [50](#r50), [52](#r52), [31](#r31)\]. Outside assessments of what the labs actually do are not encouraging. The most recent one puts no company above C+ on basic control practices \[[121](#r121), [122](#r122)\].

<span id="op2"></span>**OP2. Safeguards that survive open weights.** Once weights are released, most safeguards can be fine-tuned away \[[54](#r54), [12](#r12)\].

**Data**

<span id="op3"></span>**OP3. What filtering misses.** We do not know what a model can rebuild from the facts that remain, or retrieve in context \[[11](#r11), [12](#r12), [13](#r13)\]. Traits can also travel through data that looks clean. A teacher model's preferences, and its misalignment, pass to a student through plain number sequences \[[114](#r114)\], and sycophancy transfers through preference data in which no single example is sycophantic \[[115](#r115)\].

<span id="op4"></span>**OP4. Finding a few poisoned documents.** If a near-constant number of documents suffices, detection has to work at the level of a few hundred samples in billions \[[14](#r14)\]. Narrow secret loyalties have already been built into open-weight models in ways that evade black-box audits \[[117](#r117)\].

<span id="op5"></span>**OP5. Provenance of the corpus.** Much of the training data has no reliable record of where it came from \[[104](#r104)\].

**Pretraining**

<span id="op6"></span>**OP6. Reasoning that stays legible.** Chain-of-thought monitoring works only while models think in readable text, and architectures that reason in latent space would remove it \[[66](#r66), [105](#r105)\]. Chain-of-thought was built for capability in the first place, and whatever a model computes before its first token never appears in it \[[113](#r113)\].

**Post-training**

<span id="op7"></span>**OP7. Removing reward hacking without teaching concealment.** Penalising detected hacks may train models to hide them \[[4](#r4), [24](#r24)\].

<span id="op8"></span>**OP8. Oversight by a much weaker judge.** Debate and weak-to-strong results are promising, but nothing has been shown to work when the gap is large \[[21](#r21), [27](#r27), [28](#r28), [30](#r30)\]. Using AI to help with oversight is an obvious bet. It only works if the overseeing models cannot themselves evade evaluation \[[118](#r118), [120](#r120)\].

<span id="op9"></span>**OP9. Safety that survives fine-tuning.** Safety training is shallow enough to be undone by a little downstream tuning \[[106](#r106), [107](#r107)\].

**Evaluation**

<span id="op10"></span>**OP10. Alignment versus evaluation awareness.** Better behaviour on evals may reflect a model that knows it is being tested \[[24](#r24), [36](#r36), [37](#r37), [46](#r46)\]. The original alignment-faking setup can be reproduced outside a lab \[[127](#r127)\].

<span id="op11"></span>**OP11. Evals with stated confidence.** Elicitation and statistics are rarely standardised, and benchmark scores are rarely linked to real-world risk \[[31](#r31), [41](#r41), [42](#r42)\]. Measurement is harder still when we extrapolate whatever is easiest to measure \[[126](#r126)\].

<span id="op12"></span>**OP12. Interpretability as assurance.** Feature and circuit methods explain only part of the computation, and nobody yet knows how to turn several imperfect detectors into a guarantee \[[63](#r63), [60](#r60), [69](#r69), [72](#r72)\]. The theories of impact have been around for a while \[[123](#r123)\]. Measurable gains from them are harder to point to. Outside language models, even ranking attribution methods against each other is unsettled \[[124](#r124)\].

**Deployment and serving**

<span id="op13"></span>**OP13. Guardrails with guarantees under new attacks.** Streaming classifiers need robustness to attacks that were not in their training set \[[55](#r55), [76](#r76), [77](#r77)\].

<span id="op14"></span>**OP14. Control for long-horizon agents.** Control protocols are tested on short tasks, and monitor quality is the bottleneck \[[74](#r74), [75](#r75), [78](#r78)\]. At the labs themselves, control is only partly practised so far \[[121](#r121)\].

<span id="op15"></span>**OP15. Injected instructions and quantised weights.** The serving stack itself opens new failure modes \[[108](#r108), [109](#r109)\].

**Monitoring and iteration**

<span id="op16"></span>**OP16. A measure of monitorability.** There is no agreed metric for how much of a model's reasoning a monitor can see \[[66](#r66), [68](#r68), [45](#r45)\], and good arguments for watching internal states as well as the trace \[[113](#r113), [118](#r118)\].

<span id="op17"></span>**OP17. Misuse and incidents in the wild.** Agentic misuse can be split across many sessions so that none of them looks harmful \[[47](#r47)\]. There are no norms yet for reporting incidents \[[5](#r5)\], and independent reports of agents probing the public web have only just begun \[[119](#r119)\].

<span id="op18"></span>**OP18. Feedback and updates that do not regress.** User feedback can train in sycophancy, and silent updates can break what worked \[[110](#r110), [111](#r111)\]. Probing the assumptions a model makes about its user is one handle on it \[[116](#r116)\]. The transfer of sycophancy through preference data is another warning \[[115](#r115)\].

**Around the model**

<span id="op19"></span>**OP19. Stability and collusion among agents.** Safety evals test one agent at a time, and populations of agents have no stability analysis \[[6](#r6)\].

<span id="op20"></span>**OP20. Measuring gradual disempowerment.** Slow loss of human control has no measurement while it happens \[[79](#r79), [80](#r80), [82](#r82)\]. Secret loyalties are a sharper version of the same worry. Whether they can be found, or removed, after training is open \[[117](#r117)\].

#### Most of them are observation problems

By my rough count, fifteen of the twenty are about seeing what the model is or does. The other five are about making training or safeguards hold up.

The evidence is spread across the list. Anti-scheming training cut covert actions in o3 from 13% to 0.4%, but the models also talked more about being evaluated, and some of the drop may come from that \[[24](#r24)\]. An early Mythos Preview privately considered that it was being tested in about 29% of runs, and behaved worse when steered to believe a scenario was real \[[46](#r46), [112](#r112)\]. Opus 4.6 got better at finishing suspicious side tasks without tripping automated monitors \[[45](#r45)\]. And the reasoning trace those monitors read may not survive future training \[[66](#r66)\].

In control terms, we have decent tools for identifying a system before we close the loop, and weak ones for observing it while the loop runs.
The state we care about now includes whether the model believes it is being watched, and our measurements change that state. That is an observer design problem with a plant that adapts to the observer.
I find this framing useful mostly because control theory has decades of results on observers and robustness to borrow from. I tried a small piece of it for [safety classifiers](https://omanshuthapliyal.github.io/blog/safety-classifier-ssm/).

The course is very good at the middle of the loop. Perhaps the most useful thing it did for me was to make the rest of the loop visible.
We have learnt to examine a model before it ships. Watching it afterwards is still largely open.

#### *Afterword*: where non-frontier labs come in

Almost every reading on the list is either written by a frontier lab or about a frontier model. So what is left for the rest of us?
The course does not ask this directly, so what follows is my own reading of its resources. Most of the answer, I think, sits at the thin ends of the loop.

- **Evaluating from outside.** Much of the list is already third-party work. METR writes on reward hacking and pre-deployment risk \[[4](#r4), [32](#r32)\], Apollo on a science of evals \[[31](#r31)\], and Epoch and RAND on bio evals \[[41](#r41), [42](#r42)\]. SecureBio reviewed a lab's own chem and bio risk report, agreed with its risk levels, and still raised concerns about expert jailbreakers and users exempt from the classifiers \[[49](#r49)\]. That is what a useful outside review looks like. Others now grade the labs' practices \[[121](#r121), [122](#r122)\], and the most recent assessment finds detection and third-party assessment the strongest areas, with prevention and containment the weakest \[[121](#r121)\]. Transluce reports agent incidents it finds on the public web \[[119](#r119)\]. None of this needs a frontier model. Much of OP11 and OP17 is an outside job.
- **Domain evaluation.** The course has one reading on critical infrastructure \[[82](#r82)\], and its cyber benchmarks score exploits against ordinary software \[[39](#r39), [40](#r40)\]. Operational technology and industrial control systems have almost no evals of their own. That matters, because a failure in one system can cascade through power and water, and on to everything that depends on them \[[82](#r82)\]. The labs are unlikely to fill this gap. Industrial AI groups hold the plants and the data, and know how those systems fail. Much of that data is time series, where even evaluating interpretability methods is unsettled \[[124](#r124)\].
- **The back of the loop.** Whoever deploys a model owns its monitoring, which is where the list is thinnest. Control protocols assume a trusted, weaker model watching an untrusted one \[[74](#r74), [75](#r75)\], and the deployer is the one who has to run them. Guardrail classifiers \[[55](#r55)\] and probes \[[65](#r65), [68](#r68)\] are small models that need no frontier compute. Hallucination probes, for instance, flag made-up entities token by token and scale to 70B models \[[68](#r68)\], which is cheap enough to run beside a deployed system. Incident reporting \[[5](#r5)\] and misuse detection \[[47](#r47)\] are as much operational practice as research. On the map, this covers OP13, OP14, OP16 and OP17.
- **The front of the loop, with open models.** Filtering the pretraining data of 6.9B-parameter models gave resistance to 10,000 steps of adversarial fine-tuning \[[12](#r12)\]. That is pretraining safety research at a scale a smaller lab can afford, and it speaks to OP2 and OP3. The same paper notes that filtering does not stop a model using hazardous information supplied in context \[[12](#r12)\], so the problem is far from closed. Anyone distilling one open model into another should also read the subliminal learning work, since traits pass most easily between models that share a base \[[114](#r114)\]. The alignment-faking setup comes with a replication guide \[[127](#r127)\], and physics-minded researchers have proposed quantitative bounds on model behaviour \[[125](#r125)\].

The research agendas on the list ask for outside collaborators, mostly on monitoring and evaluation \[[87](#r87), [88](#r88), [89](#r89)\]. Research leads say the scarcest people are empirical iterators \[[86](#r86)\].

For someone coming from control, the back of the loop is perhaps the most natural place to start. Watching a system you do not fully trust, with sensors that are themselves imperfect, is an old problem there.

The middle of the loop belongs to the labs.

*The two ends are open to the rest of us.*
